01 · Basics

How a container shipment actually runs.

The sequence from booking to empty return — and the equipment choice that sits underneath it.

Most shipping problems are easier to judge once you know which stage the cargo is in. A container shipment moves through a fixed sequence, and each stage has its own deadline.

Booking. Space and equipment are reserved with the carrier for a named sailing. On busy lanes, space is committed one to three weeks ahead.

Cut-offs. Each sailing has a documentation cut-off, a VGM (verified gross mass) cut-off and a physical gate-in cut-off at the terminal. Miss any one of them and the container waits for the next vessel, even if it is standing at the gate.

Export clearance and loading. The export declaration is filed, the container is gated in, and the carrier loads it according to the stowage plan.

Ocean transit. The published transit time assumes no transhipment delay. A direct sailing and a two-transhipment routing can differ by a week or more on the same lane.

Arrival and import declaration. The import entry is filed against the bill of lading and invoice. Duty and VAT become due, unless the goods move into a bonded warehouse or under transit.

Release and delivery. The carrier releases the container once freight is paid and the original bill of lading or telex release is in place. Inland transport follows.

Empty return. The container goes back to the depot. Free time — demurrage at the terminal, detention on the box — is running from arrival, and this is where unplanned costs usually appear.

Figures are indicative and vary by carrier and box. In the Netherlands and across Europe, the road weight limit is usually the real constraint on a 20ft container, not the container's own rating — which is why heavy cargo is often split across two boxes rather than squeezed into one.

A practical ruleWork out your cargo's density first. Under roughly 400 kg per cubic metre you will run out of space before weight, so a high cube is worth the small premium. Above that, weight decides, and a 20ft is usually the cheaper answer.
02 · Volume

FCL or LCL: which one fits your cargo.

The tipping point is not only about price per cubic metre.

Full container load means the box is yours. Less than container load means your cargo is consolidated with other shipments, and deconsolidated at the destination. The choice changes your cost, your transit time and your risk.

FCL

Full container load

  • Sealed at origin, opened at destination — less handling, less damage risk
  • Faster on arrival: no deconsolidation queue
  • Predictable charges, usually quoted all-in per container
  • Cheaper per cubic metre once you fill most of the box
LCL

Less than container load

  • You pay only for the volume or weight you use
  • Sensible for small, regular or trial shipments
  • Adds three to seven days at destination for deconsolidation
  • More destination charges, quoted per shipment and per cubic metre

Where the tipping point usually sits

As a working rule, below roughly 13–15 cubic metres LCL tends to win; above that a 20ft container is often cheaper in total, even part-empty. But that comparison only holds if you count the destination side. LCL rates look low because handling, deconsolidation, documentation and delivery are charged separately once the cargo lands.

The other factor is time. An LCL shipment cannot leave the warehouse before the consolidation is full, and cannot be collected until every shipment in the container has been unpacked and checked. If a delivery date is tight, the extra days often cost more than the freight saved.

Ask for bothWhen your volume sits anywhere near the tipping point, ask us to quote FCL and LCL side by side, with destination charges included. It takes one email and it is the single clearest comparison you can make.
03 · Terms

Incoterms: who pays for what, and where risk passes.

Two questions, one term. They do not always have the same answer.

An Incoterm sets two things: how far the seller pays, and at which point the risk becomes yours. Most disputes we see come from assuming those are the same point. They often are not.

TermSeller pays toRisk passesUse it when
EXWNothing beyond making the goods availableAt the seller's premisesRarely right for containers — you need someone who can handle export in the origin country
FCAThe named place, export clearedAt that handoverThe technically correct term for containerised cargo
FOBLoading on board at the origin portOnce the goods are on boardCommon in container trade, and it gives you control of the main leg
CFR / CIFOcean freight to the destination port (CIF adds insurance)Still at origin, on boardConvenient — but the seller's agent controls the destination side
CPT / CIPCarriage to a named place, any mode (CIP adds insurance)At handover to the first carrierDoor deliveries and multimodal routings
DAPDelivery to your address, not unloaded, duty unpaidOn arrival at that addressYou want the goods delivered but will clear import yourself
DDPEverything, including import duty and VATOn deliverySeldom advisable for EU imports — the seller needs EU VAT registration

The three mistakes that cost importers money

1. Assuming cost and risk pass together. Under CIF the seller pays the freight, but the risk is yours from the moment the goods are loaded at origin. If the cargo is damaged mid-voyage, that is your claim, not theirs.

2. Not naming the place. "FOB" on its own means nothing. "FOB Ningbo, Incoterms 2020" is a term you can act on. Put it on the order and on the invoice.

3. Buying CIF and being surprised by the arrival invoice. The seller appoints the destination agent, and you pay whatever that agent charges when the container lands in Rotterdam. Those charges are not in the price you compared.

The single change that removes most surprise chargesBuy FOB or FCA and let us handle the main leg. You then control the carrier, the destination agent and the charges at arrival — and you can see what each part actually costs instead of finding out afterwards.

04 · Compliance

Customs and documentation, without the delays.

What customs expects, and the mistakes that most often hold cargo up.

Customs does not delay shipments at random. Almost every hold we see comes back to a document that is missing, inconsistent or too vague. Getting the set right before departure is far cheaper than correcting it at the terminal.

The documents you will usually need

Commercial invoiceSeller, buyer, goods, value, currency and the agreed Incoterm — stated explicitly.
Packing listPackages, weights and dimensions that match the invoice and the bill of lading.
Bill of lading or air waybillThe transport document and, for sea freight, the title to the goods.
EORI numberRequired for the importer of record in the EU. Register before the goods arrive, not after.
Certificate of originNeeded to claim a preferential duty rate under a trade agreement. No certificate, no preference.
Licences and certificatesFor food, plant products, chemicals, dual-use goods, waste and other controlled categories.
Insurance certificateRequired for CIF and CIP terms, and useful evidence in any claim.
HS classificationThe commodity code that sets the duty rate and any restrictions. Agree it before you order.

Five things that hold cargo up

1. A description that is too general. "Goods", "parts" or "samples" invites a question. Describe what it is, what it is made of and what it is for.

2. A commodity code that does not match the goods. Codes chosen for a low duty rate rarely survive an inspection, and corrections carry penalties.

3. Value and Incoterm not stated clearly. Customs needs to know what is included in the price. An invoice that does not say EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP creates an immediate query.

4. Origin evidence missing. Preferential duty is claimed at the moment of declaration. Chasing the certificate afterwards is difficult and sometimes impossible.

5. Documents that contradict each other. A weight on the packing list that differs from the bill of lading is enough to trigger a check.

05 · Planning

Where the days in a shipment go.

Plan backwards from the date the goods must be available, not forwards from the factory.

The sea leg is the longest part of a shipment but rarely the part that goes wrong. The days that get lost sit at the edges: before the cut-off and after arrival.

7–14 days before

Booking and space. Confirm equipment and sailing. On peak lanes, later bookings mean less choice and higher rates.

1–3 days before

Documentation and VGM cut-offs. These fall before the gate-in cut-off. Missing them rolls the container to the next vessel.

Transit

Sea leg. Asia to Rotterdam typically runs several weeks, and materially longer when vessels route around the Cape. Transhipment adds days.

1–3 days after arrival

Discharge and import declaration. Filed against the bill of lading and invoice. A complete document set is what keeps this short.

After release

Inland delivery. Trucking capacity in the Rotterdam region is tightest in the first days after a large vessel discharges.

Free time clock

Demurrage and detention. Usually a combined free period measured in days from discharge. After that, charges run per container per day.

Three habits that keep a plan realistic

Build in buffer at the end, not the middle. Days held in bonded storage near the port are cheap and flexible. Days lost at the cut-off are not recoverable.

Confirm the cargo ready date with your supplier in writing. A slipped ready date is the most common reason a shipment misses its booked sailing.

Know your free time before the container lands. If your warehouse cannot receive within it, arrange storage in advance — it is almost always cheaper than demurrage.

06 · Risk

Route disruption, in operational terms.

What congestion, diversions and blank sailings actually change for your cargo.

Disruption rarely stops a trade lane outright. It changes capacity, transit time and reliability — and those three then change your rate and your planning assumptions.

Blank sailings. Carriers cancel a scheduled departure to match capacity to demand. Your cargo moves to the next vessel, typically a week later, and space on that vessel tightens.

Route diversions. When vessels avoid a canal or a region, the same service takes longer and effectively removes capacity from the lane. Longer round trips mean fewer sailings and higher rates, even if nothing else changes.

Port congestion. Berth waiting time pushes discharge later, and inland trucking bunches up once a delayed vessel finally works. The container arrives, but the delivery slot does not.

Equipment imbalance. After disruption, empty containers end up in the wrong place. Equipment shortages at origin can delay a booking that otherwise had confirmed space.

Rollovers. On oversold sailings, cargo is rolled to the next vessel. Rates booked at the lowest level are usually the first to be rolled.

What you can actually do about it

Book earlier on volatile lanesCommitted space is the most reliable protection against a rollover.
Hold buffer stock for critical linesCheaper than air freight on the day the shelf runs empty.
Ask about alternative discharge portsAntwerp or Hamburg plus a short inland leg can beat waiting for Rotterdam.
Avoid tying one shipment to one deadlineSplit critical volume across two sailings where the product allows it.

07 · Cost

What actually drives your freight rate.

Why the lowest quote is often not the lowest total cost.

A freight quote is a bundle. Comparing two quotes only works when you know which parts each one includes — and which charges appear later, on a different invoice.

What sits inside a shipment's cost

Ocean freightThe carrier's base rate for the lane. The most visible part, and the most volatile.
Fuel and currency surchargesBAF and similar adjustments, usually quoted separately and revised periodically.
Terminal handlingCharged at origin and at destination. A frequent difference between two quotes.
Documentation and customs entryBill of lading issuance, declarations, certificates and any inspection follow-up.
Inland haulagePre-carriage at origin and delivery at destination, including waiting time at your site.
Demurrage and detention riskNot a quoted cost, but the one that most often turns a cheap shipment into an expensive one.
Duty, import VAT and exciseSet by classification and origin, not by your forwarder — but predictable in advance.
InsuranceCarrier liability is capped by convention and is usually far below the value of the goods.

Compare the shipment, not only the rate

An ocean-freight figure is only one part of the shipment. To compare two offers properly, check the carrier and routing, the number of transhipments, origin and destination handling, free time, inland delivery, customs support and what happens if the schedule changes.

The right choice is the option that delivers the cargo reliably within the agreed cost and time frame. A slightly higher quote can be the better value when it includes a more direct service, enough free time and a clear operational contact from booking to delivery.

08 · Practical

How to prepare a shipment request.

Ten minutes on the details below usually turns a two-day exchange into a same-day quote.

A quote is only as good as the information behind it. These are the eleven details we need before we can price a shipment properly — and the gaps that send it back to you with questions.

What to send us

Origin and destinationPort, city or full address — and whether you need port-to-port or door-to-door.
The agreed IncotermWith the place named, so we know which part of the route to price.
Cargo ready dateWhen the goods are physically available for collection, not when you hope they are.
What the goods areA plain description, plus the HS code if you already have one.
Volume and weightPackages, dimensions and gross weight — or container count and type for FCL.
How it is packedPalletised or loose, stackable or not. This decides how much fits in the box.
Special requirementsTemperature setting, dangerous goods (UN number and class), out-of-gauge dimensions.
Value of the goodsNeeded for insurance cover and for the customs value.
Importer of recordWho is importing, and their EORI number.
Your deadlineThe target delivery date, and whether it is hard or has room in it.
Services neededCustoms clearance, inland delivery, warehousing, insurance — or just the freight.

What slows a quote down

"One container, approximately." Without dimensions and gross weight we cannot confirm the equipment or check the road weight limit, so the price comes with conditions attached.

"General cargo." Not enough to classify the goods. Customs will ask, and so will the carrier if anything is restricted.

No Incoterm. We cannot tell whether you need the whole route priced or only part of it, and the two numbers are very different.

No cargo ready date. Rates and space are date-specific. An undated quote is an estimate that will change.
