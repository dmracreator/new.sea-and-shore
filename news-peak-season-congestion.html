Market update · Asia trade lanes

Peak season is still putting pressure on Asian ports.

Strong export demand and weather-related disruption are keeping vessel schedules and terminal flow under pressure. The operational effect is usually felt at the next handover, not only on the published ETA.

18 September 2026 · 4 min read
What it means
for clients

A vessel delay is rarely just a vessel delay. When terminals are congested, containers bunch up: empty equipment arrives later, cut-offs tighten, loading windows move and an originally simple delivery can run into a shortage of inland capacity.

Recent reporting on the container market points to sustained export demand from Asia alongside severe-weather disruption at Chinese ports. That combination is extending peak-season pressure and making schedules less forgiving than a normal published transit time suggests.

Plan around the handovers

For a shipment that must arrive in a fixed week, work backwards from the delivery date and include buffer for terminal gate-in, transhipment and final collection. A container that is ready at the supplier after the documentation cut-off may still miss the intended sailing.

It is also worth separating the carrier’s estimated arrival from the date your warehouse can actually receive. Customs release, terminal availability and trucking capacity sit between those two moments.

Source reporting: Seatrade Maritime reported continued peak-season tightness, severe-weather disruption at Chinese ports and high congestion levels on 17 September. Read the original report ↗

What to do now

Confirm cargo-ready dates early, send documents before the cut-off, and reserve inland delivery capacity as soon as discharge becomes likely. If the delivery date is firm, ask us to check the next two sailings rather than relying on a single schedule.
