Market update · Asia–Europe routing

Red Sea routing is returning gradually, not all at once.

Some Asia–Europe capacity is moving back through the Red Sea, but services remain mixed. For importers, the route written on a quote matters more than any general claim that transit times are “back to normal”.

04 September 2026 · 4 min read
What it means
for clients

A Cape routing and a Suez routing do not only have different transit times. They affect equipment rotation, schedule reliability, connections at transhipment ports and the space available on the next departure.

Freightos reported that some alliance services were returning through the Red Sea, while a sizeable share of Asia–Europe capacity continued to use longer alternatives. This is progress, but it is not a single network-wide switch.

Check the actual service

For urgent cargo, ask which port pairs, vessels and transhipments sit behind the quote. The answer may change sailing by sailing. If a supplier is planning production around a previous transit time, reset that assumption before the goods are ready.

For regular cargo, a mix of routings can be useful when it protects a delivery plan. The key is to make the trade-off explicit: the fastest option, the most stable option and the lowest-cost option may not be the same booking.

Source reporting: Freightos reported in its September outlook that a portion of Asia–Europe capacity was returning through the Red Sea, while routing and congestion conditions remained mixed. Read the original outlook ↗

What to ask before booking

Ask for the planned route, number of transhipments, expected port of discharge, free time and the next available alternative. We can put those options beside each other and explain what the difference means for your cargo-ready date and delivery target.
